\chapter{Results}
\label{cha:results}

In this chapter, we present the results obtained from the fire risk classification 
experiments. The analysis is structured to evaluate the performance of individual 
models before moving to the ensemble approach. The primary goal is to assess the 
ability of different architectures to classify fire risk levels accurately across 
various input configurations and training strategies.

We evaluate three deep learning architectures individually:
\begin{itemize}
    \item ResNet50
    \item DenseNet161
    \item EfficientNet-B5
\end{itemize}

Each model is tested using three different input configurations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item IRG images (Infrared, Red, Green)
    \item RGB images (Red, Green, Blue)
    \item RGB images with NDVI as a fourth channel (Normalized Difference Vegetation Index)
\end{itemize}

These configurations allow us to determine the most effective input format 
for fire risk classification. The models are trained across multiple learning 
rates and weight decays using the AdamW optimizer, which is widely used in 
deep learning due to its improved stability and convergence properties compared 
to traditional optimizers. Given its effectiveness, we did not experiment with 
alternative optimizers. If the results for an architecture are unsatisfactory, 
we further experiment with a gradual unfreezing strategy, as is the case for 
DenseNet161. It is also important to note that the models results are averaged
over three runs to ensure robustness and reliability.

Following the evaluation of individual architectures, we present the results of 
different ensemble models combining ResNet50, DenseNet161, and EfficientNet-B5 to 
leverage the strengths of each network. The ensemble approach is expected to enhance 
classification performance by integrating complementary decision-making processes 
from the individual models.

Finally, we analyze failure cases in the ensemble model's final results. Specifically, 
we focus on instances where all models in the ensemble incorrectly predict an average 
fire risk when, in reality, a very high fire risk is present. Understanding these 
failure cases is crucial for improving model robustness and minimizing false negatives 
in real-world applications.

As seen in Figure \ref{fig:r50_results}, models trained with higher learning rates 
show more unstable validation loss. In particular, $1\times10^{-4}$ shows significant 
fluctuations, indicating instability and potential overfitting. Despite achieving lower 
training loss, its validation accuracy stagnates, reinforcing the idea that overly 
aggressive learning rates lead to poor generalization.

In contrast, lower learning rates ($1\times10^{-5}$ and $5\times10^{-6}$) demonstrate 
smoother and more stable validation loss trends. This suggests that smaller 
learning rates enable a more controlled optimization process, reducing drastic 
fluctuations and improving generalization. Overall, the learning rates of $1\times10^{-5}$ 
and $5\times10^{-6}$ appear to have the best balance between validation and training performance.

One key observation from Table \ref{tab:resnet50_irg} is that lower learning 
rates tend to yield better validation performance. A learning rate of

This suggests that larger learning rates may cause the model to converge too quickly to 
suboptimal solutions, limiting generalization.

Interestingly, the lowest learning rate ($5\times10^{-6}$) achieves the highest test 
accuracy (\textbf{80.82\%}), indicating that very small learning rates may facilitate 
more stable convergence. However, its validation accuracy does not match that of 
$1\times10^{-5}$, which suggests a possible trade-off between validation and test performance.

may not have been sufficient for effectively leveraging the NDVI information.

of a fourth channel may not be fully leveraged by the initial convolutional layers.

After reviewing these two cases (Figure \ref{fig:false_positives}), both exhibit visual characteristics 
strongly associated with high fire risk, despite being labeled as \textbf{Average Risk} in the dataset.

In the first example, the property is closely surrounded by dense vegetation, including trees and

In the second example, unlike the first case where vegetation proximity is the primary concern, 
this location features widespread, uninterrupted vegetation cover. According to Table \ref{fig:fire-risk-table}, 
extensive vegetation continuity is a critical parameter for extreme fire risk, which likely contributed 
to the ensemble model's classification of Very High Risk.

Another observations is that shadows present in the imagery may have influenced the model's perception of 
vegetation density, potentially leading to an overestimation of fire risk. In areas where dense tree 
canopies cast dark shadows, the model might interpret these regions as continuous vegetation cover rather 
than open or managed spaces. This effect could have contributed to the ensemble's misclassification of certain 
as Very High Risk despite their actual fire exposure conditions.
