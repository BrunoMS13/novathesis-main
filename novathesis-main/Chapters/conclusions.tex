\chapter{Conclusions}
\label{cha:conclusions}

This study has demonstrated the potential of deep learning techniques for automating 
fire risk classification using aerial imagery. By leveraging convolutional neural 
networks (CNNs) such as ResNet-50, DenseNet-161, and EfficientNet-B5, we have shown 
that deep learning models can effectively classify vegetation-related fire risks with 
high accuracy.

The results indicate that ensemble models, particularly those incorporating multiple 
spectral inputs (RGB, IRG, and NDVI), provide improved generalization and classification 
robustness compared to individual architectures. The Multi-Input Ensemble achieved the 
highest performance, with an accuracy of \textbf{86.53\%} and an F1-score of \textbf{86.53\%}, 
demonstrating the effectiveness of combining diverse input sources and model architectures. 
Additionally, the RGB-Only Ensemble also delivered strong results, achieving an accuracy of \textbf{84.97\%}
and an F1-score of \textbf{84.99\%}, showing that even without additional spectral data, 
a well-optimized CNN model can achieve high classification performance.

Despite these advancements, some limitations remain. Some misclassification cases between
categories, suggest that further refinement in labeled data could be necessary.

For future work, several avenues can be explored. Enhancing data augmentation techniques 
could improve model generalization, particularly if a more granular multi-class classification 
approach is adopted or if a larger, more balanced dataset becomes available. This would help 
mitigate biases and improve the model's ability to distinguish between subtle variations in 
fire risk levels.

Developing semi-supervised or self-supervised learning approaches could reduce reliance on 
extensive labeled datasets, allowing the model to adapt to different geographical regions 
with minimal manual intervention. This would be especially beneficial in expanding the 
system's applicability beyond the dataset used in this study.

Additionally, integrating temporal satellite imagery could enable dynamic risk assessments 
by accounting for seasonal changes in vegetation density and evolving fire hazards. This 
would allow for a more proactive approach to fire prevention by continuously monitoring 
landscape changes that affect fire susceptibility.

Lastly, optimizing model efficiency for real-time inference is crucial for practical 
deployment, particularly in emergency response scenarios. Reducing computational overhead 
while maintaining high accuracy would make the system more accessible for integration into 
early warning systems and real-time decision-making tools used by authorities and first 
responders.

In conclusion, this research contributes to the growing field of AI-driven environmental 
monitoring by providing a scalable and efficient method for fire risk classification. 
Automating risk assessment enables faster and more accurate wildfire prevention strategies, 
improving response times and reducing the impact of climate change-induced disasters.
