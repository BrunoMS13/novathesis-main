\chapter{Related Work}
\label{cha:related-work}


Classifying housing fire risk using remote sensing imagery presents unique 
challenges, including environmental variability, fine-grained spatial features, 
and high interclass similarity. Deep learning, particularly convolutional neural
networks (CNNs), has demonstrated strong potential in extracting semantic 
features from complex visual data, making it highly suitable for this domain. 

This chapter reviews three prominent CNN architectures, ResNet-50, DenseNet-161,
and EfficientNet-B5, that have shown outstanding performance in image classification 
tasks. It also examines how these models have been applied in remote sensing 
contexts and highlights ensemble-based strategies that further improve 
classification robustness in high-resolution, complex datasets.

\section{Deep CNN Architectures}
\subsection{ResNet-50 Architecture}


ResNet-50 \cite{he2016deep} is a deep convolutional neural network from the ResNet family, 
which introduced residual learning to significantly enhance gradient propagation 
in deep architectures. The key innovation in ResNet models is the use of residual 
connections, which allow the network to learn residual functions rather than direct 
mappings. This mitigates the vanishing gradient problem and enables the training of 
very deep networks.

ResNet-50 consists of 50 layers, primarily composed of residual blocks. Each residual 
block incorporates identity shortcuts that bypass multiple convolutional layers, 
facilitating gradient flow and allowing for deeper feature learning without the risk of 
accuracy degradation.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{../Figures/residual_block.png}
    \caption{Representation of a residual block used in ResNet-50 from \cite{he2016deep}.}
    \label{fig:resnet_block}
\end{figure}

The architecture follows a structured layout, beginning with an initial convolutional 
layer followed by multiple residual blocks. Each residual block as seen in Figure \ref{fig:resnet_block}
consists of a bottleneck 
structure with three convolutional layers: a $1 \times 1$ convolution for dimensionality 
reduction, a $3 \times 3$ convolution for feature extraction, and another $1 \times 1$ 
convolution for dimensionality restoration. Shortcut connections then add the original 
input back to the transformed output, improving feature reuse and enabling efficient 
deep learning.

The model takes an input of size $224 \times 224 \times 3$ and progressively reduces 
spatial dimensions while increasing feature depth. A final global average pooling 
layer extracts high-level features before passing them to a fully connected layer for classification.

A major advantage of ResNet-50 is its ability to train deep models efficiently 
without suffering from performance degradation. The residual connections enable 
stable gradient flow, reducing the risk of vanishing gradients and improving 
convergence speed. These properties make ResNet-50 particularly effective for 
large-scale image classification tasks.

Additionally, ResNet-50 incorporates batch normalization and ReLU activation to enhance 
training stability and efficiency. Its hierarchical structure captures increasingly 
complex patterns with depth, making it well-suited for high-resolution image analysis.

ResNet-50 has demonstrated strong performance on benchmark datasets such as 
ImageNet and has become a standard architecture in deep learning applications. 
Its combination of depth, residual learning, and efficient feature extraction 
makes it a powerful model for image classification, object detection, and other 
computer vision tasks.


\subsection{DenseNet-161 Architecture}

DenseNet-161 \cite{huang2017densely} is a deep convolutional neural network from the DenseNet family, 
designed to optimize feature reuse and improve information flow between layers. 
The core principle of DenseNet is dense connectivity, where each layer receives 
input from all preceding layers, ensuring more effective gradient propagation 
and reducing feature redundancy.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{../Figures/DensenetArchitect.png}
    \caption{Representation of DenseNet with three dense blocks from \cite{DensenetResearchGate2025}.}
    \label{fig:densenet_block}
\end{figure}

A defining characteristic of DenseNet-161 is the use of dense blocks, each 
consisting of multiple convolutional layers that share information through 
direct connections, as illustrated in Fig. \ref{fig:densenet_block}. These dense 
connections alleviate the vanishing gradient problem and enable the model to 
achieve high accuracy with fewer parameters compared to traditional deep architectures. 
Between these dense blocks, transition layers are used to control feature map growth, 
reducing computational cost while maintaining effective feature extraction.

DenseNet-161 follows a structured layout, beginning with an initial convolutional 
layer followed by four dense blocks. Each dense block consists of multiple layers 
applying $3 \times 3$ convolutions with batch normalization and ReLU activation. 
Within a dense block, each layer concatenates its feature maps with those of all 
preceding layers, increasing the model's representational capacity. Between the 
dense blocks, transition layers with $1 \times 1$ convolutions and average pooling 
help control dimensionality and prevent excessive memory consumption.

The model takes an input of size $224 \times 224 \times 3$ and progressively 
reduces spatial dimensions while increasing feature depth. The final global 
average pooling layer extracts high-level features, which are then passed to 
a fully connected classification layer.

One of DenseNet-161's key advantages is its efficient parameter utilization. 
Unlike traditional deep networks, which may contain redundant parameters, DenseNet 
maximizes feature reuse through its densely connected layers, leading to improved
accuracy with fewer parameters. This makes it particularly effective for applications 
requiring robust feature extraction with limited computational resources.

Additionally, DenseNet-161 incorporates residual-like connections without summation, 
preserving fine-grained information across layers. The inclusion of transition layers 
ensures that the number of feature maps does not grow excessively, keeping computational 
costs manageable while maintaining high representational capacity.

Due to its strong performance on benchmark datasets such as ImageNet, DenseNet-161 
has been widely adopted in image classification tasks. Its dense connectivity, batch 
normalization, and efficient pooling mechanisms make it well-suited for tasks that 
require deep feature learning. In this study, we leverage DenseNet-161's strengths 
to enhance housing fire risk classification, capitalizing on its superior feature extraction 
capabilities to improve predictive performance.



\subsection{EfficientNet-B5 Architecture}

EfficientNet-B5 \cite{efficientnet} is a deep convolutional neural network from the EfficientNet family, 
designed to achieve high accuracy while maintaining computational efficiency. Unlike 
traditional deep networks that scale depth, width, or resolution independently, 
EfficientNet models employ compound scaling, which balances these three factors proportionally. 
This results in a more optimized architecture that improves accuracy without a 
significant increase in computational cost.

The core principle behind compound scaling is that increasing the network's depth 
(number of layers), width (number of channels per layer), and resolution (input image size) 
together leads to more efficient model scaling compared to arbitrarily increasing one 
of these dimensions. EfficientNet-B5 follows this principle by scaling up EfficientNet-B0 
using pre-determined coefficients, resulting in a larger, more accurate model with a 
manageable computational requirement.


EfficientNet-B5 utilizes Mobile Inverted Bottleneck Convolution (MBConv) blocks, 
originally introduced in MobileNetV2 \cite{mobilenetv2}. These blocks employ depthwise separable 
convolutions, reducing the number of parameters while preserving feature extraction 
efficiency. Each MBConv block consists of:
\begin{itemize}
    \item An expansion phase to increase dimensionality,
    \item A depthwise convolution for spatial feature extraction,
    \item A projection phase to reduce dimensionality while maintaining essential information.
\end{itemize}
Additionally, squeeze-and-excitation (SE) blocks are integrated into each MBConv block, 
enabling the network to adaptively recalibrate feature maps by learning channel-wise attention.

The network begins with a standard convolutional layer, followed by multiple MBConv 
blocks, progressively increasing feature depth while reducing spatial resolution. The 
final layers consist of a global average pooling operation followed by a fully 
connected classification layer.

One of the key advantages of EfficientNet-B5 is its ability to achieve high accuracy 
while maintaining computational efficiency. Unlike traditional deep networks, which 
often increase parameters disproportionately, EfficientNet achieves state-of-the-art 
performance on benchmark datasets with significantly fewer parameters and FLOPs.

Additionally, EfficientNet-B5 incorporates the following:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Swish activation functions to improve gradient flow,
    \item Batch normalization for enhanced training stability,
    \item Squeeze-and-excitation (SE) blocks for adaptive feature recalibration.
\end{itemize}
These enhancements enable EfficientNet-B5 to achieve superior accuracy on higher resolution 
images ($456 \times 456$), leading to better feature extraction for fine-grained classification tasks.

EfficientNet-B5 has consistently outperformed many traditional deep networks on ImageNet 
and other large-scale classification benchmarks, demonstrating its effectiveness in real-world 
applications. Its combination of depthwise separable convolutions, squeeze-and-excitation blocks, 
and compound scaling makes it one of the most efficient architectures for high-accuracy 
image classification. These properties make EfficientNet-B5 a strong candidate for fire 
risk classification, as it balances computational efficiency with strong feature extraction 
capabilities.


\section{CNNs in Remote Sensing Applications}

Deep learning has become the dominant approach for remote sensing image classification, 
leveraging convolutional neural networks (\gls{cnn}) to extract spatial and spectral features 
from aerial and satellite imagery. While significant progress has been made in classifying 
land-use and scene categories, housing fire risk classification remains a challenging task due 
to its dependence on vegetation conditions, environmental variations, and fine-grained 
differences between risk levels.

The article \textit{Remote Sensing Image Scene Classification Meets Deep Learning} 
\cite{cheng2020remote} provides a systematic review of deep learning methods for remote 
sensing image classification, covering more than 160 papers. It compares the accuracies 
of several models using benchmark datasets, including UC-MERCED \cite{yang2010bag}, AID 
\cite{xia2017aid}, and NWPU-RESISC45 \cite{cheng2017remote}.

The UC-Merced dataset contains 2100 images across 21 land-use classes, with a resolution of 
256x256 pixels at 0.3 m spatial resolution. The AID dataset expands on this, featuring 10,000 
images from 30 scene classes, varying in resolution from 8 m to 0.5 m per pixel. NWPU-RESISC45 
is the largest, with 31,500 images spanning 45 categories, covering over 100 countries, with
pixel resolutions ranging from 30 m to 0.2 m.

Although these datasets focus on general land-use and scene classification, they share similarities 
with housing fire risk classification, particularly in dealing with heterogeneous environments and high 
interclass similarity. In both cases, distinguishing between classes can be challenging due to 
factors such as subtle differences in vegetation density, variations in terrain, and seasonal 
changes affecting the imagery.

\subsection{Deep Learning-Based Scene Classification Algorithms}

Scene classification using deep learning has gained significant attention in remote sensing research. 
The paper \cite{cheng2020remote} categorizes deep learning approaches into three main types: autoencoder-based methods, 
CNN-based methods, and GAN-based methods. While all three have been explored for remote sensing applications, 
CNN-based approaches have demonstrated the highest performance and practical applicability.

\subsubsection{Autoencoder-Based Methods}
Autoencoders have been widely used in earlier years for remote sensing classification due to their ability 
to automatically learn mid-level feature representations from unlabeled data. However, their performance 
is generally inferior to CNN-based approaches, as they do not fully leverage scene class information 
for optimal feature discrimination. Given these limitations, autoencoder-based methods 
are not considered in this study.

\subsubsection{CNN-Based Methods}
CNN-based approaches have become the dominant technique for remote sensing image classification, offering 
state-of-the-art accuracy across various datasets. These methods can be divided into three categories:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pretrained \gls{cnn} as Feature Extractors:} \gls{cnn} pretrained on large datasets such as ImageNet are used to extract 
    deep features from remote sensing images, which are then fed into a separate classifier (e.g., SVM).
    \item \textbf{Fine-Tuning Pretrained \gls{cnn}:} A CNN pretrained on ImageNet is fine-tuned on the target dataset, adjusting its 
    learned features for improved performance.
    \item \textbf{Training \gls{cnn} from Scratch:} A CNN is trained from the ground up using a custom dataset, requiring 
    significantly more labeled samples and computational resources.
\end{itemize}

Among these, fine-tuning pretrained \gls{cnn} has shown the best trade-off between accuracy and computational efficiency, 
particularly for remote sensing applications with limited labeled data. Fine-tuning approaches such as SF-CNN 
(Scale-Free CNN) \cite{SF-CNN} and GBN (Gated Bidirectional Network) \cite{GBN} have achieved accuracies above 98\% 
on benchmark datasets.

For larger-scale and more complex classification tasks, ensemble models like Hydra \cite{hydra} have been
shown to outperform single \gls{cnn}. Hydra employs an ensemble of ResNet and DenseNet architectures,
fine-tuning multiple \gls{cnn} separately and combining their outputs through a \textbf{majority voting strategy}.
An image is classified into the majority class if at least half of the models agree; otherwise, it is labeled as unknown.
This approach allows for improved 
generalization and robustness, particularly in datasets with high interclass similarity.

\paragraph{Hydra Framework}
This framework follows an ensemble-based approach designed to improve classification accuracy while reducing training time. 
The process consists of two main stages:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item An initial CNN is trained and coarsely optimized, forming the "Hydra's body."
    \item The obtained weights are then fine-tuned multiple times using different augmentation techniques, region crop styles, 
    and class weighting strategies to create the ensemble’s individual models, referred to as the "Hydra's heads."
\end{enumerate}

By leveraging this strategy, the ensemble encourages diversity among the individual models, which improves the robustness of predictions. 
A visualization of the Hydra framework is presented in Figure \ref{fig:hydra-testing}.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{../Figures/hydra-testing.png}
    \caption{Hydra framework testing flowchart from \cite{hydra}.}
    \label{fig:hydra-testing}
\end{figure}

The training procedure ensures that each head of the Hydra converges to a different local optimum by employing various 
augmentation techniques such as online data augmentation, different crop strategies, and metadata inclusion. This ensemble 
approach mitigates the limitations of using a single CNN and has demonstrated superior classification performance in remote sensing applications.

At inference time, an input image is passed through all heads of the Hydra, and each head provides a classification score. 
The final prediction is determined through majority voting, which enhances the ensemble’s robustness against individual model biases.

\paragraph{Limitations of Fine-Tuning Pretrained \gls{cnn}}
Despite their effectiveness, fine-tuning pretrained \gls{cnn} introduces certain challenges. Pretrained models are typically optimized for generic 
image classification tasks (e.g., natural images from ImageNet), which may not fully align with the spectral and spatial characteristics of 
remote sensing images. Furthermore, pretrained models may introduce biases based on the original training data, necessitating careful 
selection of hyperparameters and augmentation techniques.

\subsubsection{GAN-Based Methods}
Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) have also been explored as an alternative approach to remote sensing classification, 
offering the ability to generate synthetic samples for training deep learning models \cite{attgan, martagan}.  
While GAN-based techniques can help mitigate data scarcity, their application in scene classification remains limited.

Compared to CNN-based methods, GANs face two primary drawbacks: (1) they typically require additional classifiers 
for training, making them less efficient as stand-alone classification models, and (2) their overall classification 
accuracy tends to be lower than that of CNN-based approaches. Given these constraints, GAN-based methods are not 
considered in this study.

\section{Conclusion}

Deep learning has significantly advanced remote sensing image classification, with CNN-based 
approaches achieving state-of-the-art performance. Among the different strategies explored 
in the literature, fine-tuning pretrained CNNs has proven to be the most effective for tasks 
with limited labeled data, offering a strong balance between computational efficiency and accuracy.

Ensemble learning methods, such as the Hydra framework, have further demonstrated their 
effectiveness in handling complex classification problems. By leveraging multiple CNN 
architectures and combining their outputs through majority voting, ensemble models enhance 
generalization and reduce the impact of individual model biases. This approach is 
particularly relevant to housing fire risk classification, where subtle differences between 
risk categories and interclass similarity can pose significant challenges.

Given these findings, this study will explore the application of an ensemble-based 
method for housing fire risk classification. By integrating models such as ResNet-50, DenseNet-161, 
and EfficientNet-B5, we aim to leverage the strengths of each architecture to improve 
prediction robustness. A \textbf{majority voting strategy} will be employed to ensure stable 
and reliable classifications, mitigating potential biases from individual models.
