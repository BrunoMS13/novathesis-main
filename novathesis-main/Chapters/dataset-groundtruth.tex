\chapter{Dataset and Ground Truth}
\label{cha:dataset-groundtruth}

In the previous chapter, we discussed the data collection process and the augmentation 
techniques applied to increase the dataset size. In this chapter, we analyze and discuss 
the ground truth provided by the \gls{adai} study.

The \gls{adai} study provided fire risk classifications for exactly 1923 isolated houses 
in Portugal, which we use as the ground truth for our model. These houses are classified 
into five fire risk categories, as shown in Table \ref{fig:fire-risk-table}. The 
distribution of classifications is presented in Table \ref{fig:data-distribution}:

\begin{table}[ht]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{|c|c|}
        \hline
        \textbf{Fire Risk} & \textbf{Number of Houses} \\ \hline
        Low                &  25  \\ \hline
        Moderate           &  210  \\ \hline
        High               &  781  \\ \hline
        Very High          &  763  \\ \hline
        Extreme            &  151  \\ \hline
        Total              &  1923 \\ \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Fire risk classification distribution.}
    \label{fig:data-distribution}
\end{table}

To facilitate model training and improve predictive performance, we consolidated 
the five original fire risk categories into two broader classes: Lower Risk 
(which includes Low, Moderate, and High categories) and Higher Risk (which includes 
Very High and Extreme categories). This transformation was primarily motivated 
by the need to create a more balanced dataset, ensuring that the model does not 
become disproportionately biased toward the larger categories. With the new 
grouping, the dataset achieves a more even distribution, containing 1016 houses 
in the Lower Risk class and 914 in the Higher Risk class, making it more suitable 
for machine learning applications.

A significant issue with the original five-category classification was that certain 
classes contained considerably more data points than others, potentially leading to 
biased model behavior. Machine learning models tend to favor dominant classes, 
meaning that in an imbalanced dataset, predictions may be skewed towards categories 
with more training examples, while underrepresented categories are more likely 
to be misclassified. By consolidating the fire risk levels into two broader groups, 
we mitigate this issue and improve the model's ability to generalize effectively.

Additionally, some fire risk levels can be difficult to tell apart, especially when 
relying only on aerial imagery. As shown in Figure \ref{fig:fire-risk-table}, certain 
differences, like how shrubs are grouped in the 5-50m surroundings to differentiate
between high and very high risk, may not be obvious from a top-down view.

While a five-class classification may provide more granularity in risk assessment, 
the complexity of distinguishing these finer details, combined with the limitations 
of aerial imaging makes the two-class system a more practical and reliable approach 
given the available dataset. A more refined multi-class classification could become 
viable with higher-resolution imaging, additional geospatial data, or a larger 
dataset with more balanced category distributions. However, with the current data 
constraints, consolidating fire risk into two primary classes strikes a balance 
between interpretability, dataset balance, and model effectiveness.

\section{Ground Truth Collection}
The data provided by the \gls{adai} study is stored in a .kmz file format, which 
can be opened using Google Earth. Through Google Earth, we can visualize the 
exact locations of the classified houses and their respective fire risk levels, 
as illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:gearth_vis}. The classifications are 
color-coded: blue represents low risk, green represents moderate risk, yellow 
represents high risk, orange represents very high risk, and red represents 
extreme risk (as seen in figure \ref{fig:fire-risk-table}).

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{../Figures/GoogleEarthVis.png}
    \caption{Visualization of classified houses in Braga using Google Earth.}
    \label{fig:gearth_vis}
\end{figure}

To extract and utilize this data, a Python script was developed to parse the 
.kmz file, retrieving the coordinates and fire risk classifications of the houses. 
The extracted data is then stored in a .csv file, which is subsequently used by
the data collection script to fetch corresponding aerial imagery from the \gls{dgt} 
orthophoto service.
