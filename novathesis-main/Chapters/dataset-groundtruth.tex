\chapter{Dataset and Ground Truth}
\label{cha:dataset-groundtruth}

In the previous chapter, we discussed the data collection process and the augmentation 
techniques applied to increase the dataset size. This chapter analyzes the ground truth 
fire risk classifications provided by the \gls{adai} study.

The \gls{adai} study assigned fire risk classifications to 1923 isolated houses in Portugal, 
which serve as the ground truth for our model. These houses were categorized into five 
fire risk levels, as shown in Table \ref{fig:data-distribution}.

To facilitate model training and improve predictive performance, we consolidated 
the five fire risk levels into two broader categories: Average Risk (Low, Moderate, 
and High) and Very High Risk (Very High and Extreme). This transformation was 
primarily motivated by the need to balance the dataset, preventing the model from 
becoming overly biased toward the more frequent classes. After reclassification, the 
dataset contains 1016 houses in the Average Risk category and 914 houses in 
the Very High Risk category, making it more suitable for machine learning applications.

A major challenge with the original five-category classification was class imbalance. 
Machine learning models tend to favor dominant classes, leading to skewed predictions 
and increased misclassification rates for underrepresented categories. By consolidating 
fire risk levels into two groups, we mitigate this issue and improve the model's ability 
to generalize.

Additionally, some fire risk levels are difficult to distinguish based solely on aerial 
imagery. As shown in Figure \ref{fig:fire-risk-table}, certain differences, such as 
the arrangement of vegetation within a 5-50m radius, are subtle and may not be 
easily visible from a top-down perspective.

While a five-class classification offers greater granularity, the complexity of 
distinguishing these finer details combined with the limitations of aerial 
imaging makes a two-class system a more practical and reliable approach for the 
available dataset. A more refined multi-class classification could become viable with 
a more balanced dataset. 
However, given current constraints, consolidating fire risk into two primary categories 
strikes a balance between interpretability, dataset balance, and model effectiveness.


\section{Challenges in Fire Risk Classification}

Despite the dataset's structured fire risk classifications, several challenges arise 
that can impact model performance.  

One of the primary difficulties is interclass similarity, certain fire risk levels, such 
as High and Very High risk, may have overlapping vegetation characteristics. Since the 
classification criteria often depend on fine-grained details such as vegetation density 
and its proximity to houses, distinguishing between adjacent fire risk levels can be 
difficult, even for human annotators.

Additionally, imaging conditions introduce further complexity. As shown in 
Figure \ref{fig:veryhigh-vs-high}, orthophotos captured at different times of the day 
exhibit lighting variations and shadows, which can obscure vegetation details.  
These variations can affect the appearance of fire-prone areas, potentially making 
some regions seem more or less at risk than they actually are.

visual similarities between fire risk levels and generalize across different lighting 
conditions. To mitigate these issues, data augmentation techniques discussed in \ref{cha:methodology} 
were applied to improve the model's ability to recognize fire risk features 
under various conditions.



\section{Ground Truth Collection}

The fire risk classifications provided by the \gls{adai} study were stored in a `.kmz` 
file format, which can be viewed using Google Earth. This format allows for the 
visualization of the exact locations of classified houses along with their assigned 
fire risk levels, as illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:gearth_vis}. The classifications 
are color-coded: blue represents low risk, green represents moderate risk, yellow 
represents high risk, orange represents very high risk, and red represents extreme 
risk, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:fire-risk-table}.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{../Figures/GoogleEarthVis.png}
    \caption{Visualization of classified houses in Braga using Google Earth.}
    \label{fig:gearth_vis}
\end{figure}

To extract and utilize this data, a Python script was developed to parse the `.kmz` 
file and retrieve the coordinates and fire risk classifications of each house. 
The extracted data was stored in a `.csv` file, which was then used by the data 
collection script to fetch corresponding aerial imagery from the \gls{dgt} orthophoto 
service.
