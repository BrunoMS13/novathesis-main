\chapter{Methodology}
\label{cha:methodology}

In this chapter, we explain the methodology used in the development of this work. We begin 
by detailing how the data was obtained and the logic behind its selection. Additionally, we 
present the data augmentation techniques applied to the dataset and describe the methodology 
employed in training the models.

\section{Data Collection}
The \gls{dgt} has conducted an extensive aerial photogrammetric survey of Continental Portugal. 
This initiative resulted in the creation of orthophotos from 2018 to 2021, providing a high-resolution 
dataset suitable for geographic and environmental analysis.

\subsection{Technical Specifications}
The orthophotos possess the following characteristics:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Spatial Resolution:} 25 cm/pixel.
    \item \textbf{Spectral Resolution:} Four-band imagery including Red, Green, Blue (RGB) and Near Infrared (NIR), particularly useful for vegetation analysis.
    \item \textbf{Geographic Coverage:} The entirety of Continental Portugal.
    \item \textbf{Reference System:} 
        \begin{itemize}
            \item \textbf{EPSG:3763} - Projected coordinate system optimized for accurate distance measurements, ideal for mapping, surveying, and GIS applications in Portugal.
            \item \textbf{EPSG:4258} - Geographic coordinate system primarily used for geodetic applications and alignment across Europe.
        \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

The orthophotos are publicly accessible through a Web Map Tile Service (WMTS), allowing 
seamless integration into research and application development. Our dataset consists of 
RGB and IRG orthophotos extracted from this open data source. To facilitate extraction,
we developed a Python module that retrieves images based on bounding box coordinates encompassing 
residential locations within the dataset. A total of 1929 RGB and IRG images were utilized.

For the fire risk assessment, we defined a 62.5-meter radius to ensure that the assessment 
covers houses within at least 50 meters. The remaining 12.5 meters accounts for the houses 
themselves. This is, of course, an approximation, as houses vary in shape and size. 
Consequently, each data entry is represented as a 500x500 pixel image.

\section{Data Augmentation}
To enhance the dataset, we applied various data augmentation techniques. These augmentations 
introduce diversity to the dataset, improving the model's generalization ability and mitigating 
overfitting, a crucial factor given the dataset's relatively small size and the complexity of 
the problem. Without augmentation, the models risk memorizing training data rather than learning 
underlying patterns. Given the complexity of the classification task, more advanced models are required 
to capture the intricate relationships in the data. However, these complex models have a higher 
capacity and tend to overfit more easily when trained on a small dataset. Instead of generalizing 
to unseen data, they start memorizing specific details of the training images, leading to poor 
performance on new samples. By applying data augmentation, we introduce variations that help the 
model learn robust features instead of relying on exact memorization.

As shown in Table \ref{tab:augmentations}, we applied four different augmentation techniques 
\cite{torchvision2016}. Horizontal and vertical flips
were applied with a probability of 50\%, ensuring that vegetation appears in different 
orientations relative to the house. Random rotations within a $[-30^{\circ}, 30^{\circ}]$ 
range further increase variability, enabling the model to better recognize patterns. Additionally, 
random brightness adjustment within $[-20\%, 20\%]$ was introduced to account for variations 
in lighting conditions, as images were captured at different times of the day and under varying cloud cover.

It is important to note that although the same augmentation techniques are applied to both 
RGB and IRG images, their application remains random during training. Consequently, no two 
training instances are identical. Additionally, some augmentation parameters, such as brightness 
variation, may behave differently between the two formats, further increasing dataset variability.




\section{Training, validation and testing.}

In this section it will be explained the methodology behind the training, validation and testing of the
chosen models, leaving the discussion of the results for a different chapter.

\subsection{Environment Setup}

To ensure a stable and reproducible training environment, we utilized Poetry \cite{poetry2023} for 
dependency management and a Docker \cite{docker2023} container with CUDA support for execution 
on the NOVA DI-Cluster. This approach allows for efficient resource management while leveraging GPU acceleration
for faster training.

The training was performed on the \textit{alakazam} nodes of the cluster, which are equipped with
the following specifications:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{CPU:} Dual AMD EPYC 9124 (64 threads total)
    \item \textbf{GPU:} NVIDIA L4 (23GB VRAM, CUDA 12.0)
    \item \textbf{Memory:} 251GB RAM
\end{itemize}

The Docker container accepts several command-line arguments that allow for flexible 
control over the training process, some of the most important and used are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{--train} and \texttt{--test}: Specify whether to train a new model or evaluate an existing one.
    \item \texttt{--algorithm}: Choose the model architecture to train or test.
    \item \texttt{--num\_epochs} and \texttt{--batch\_size}: Define the number of training epochs and batch size per iteration.
    \item \texttt{--lr} and \texttt{--wd}: Control the initial learning rate and weight decay for optimization.
    \item \texttt{--load\_weights}: Allows loading pre-trained model weights for fine-tuning.
    \item \texttt{--save\_as}: Defines the output filename for storing the trained model and metrics.
    \item \texttt{--num\_classes}: Specifies the number of classes for classification.
    \item \texttt{--ndvi}: Enables the use of a fourth channel for the NDVI index in the input images.
\end{itemize}

By configuring these parameters at runtime, we ensure flexibility while maintaining consistency 
across different training runs. This setup enables efficient experimentation without modifying 
the core training script.

\subsection{Training and validation}

Firstly, the dataset is split into a training and testing set using 
a 90\% / 10\% ratio, resulting in approximately 1736 training samples and 
193 testing samples. The training set is then further divided into training 
and validation sets with an 80\% / 20\% ratio to assess the model's learning 
capabilities during training. This results in 1388 non-augmented training samples and 
348 validation samples.

With the application of data augmentation techniques, horizontal and vertical flips alone 
generate 4x more variations, while rotation and brightness jittering create an effectively 
infinite number of unique transformations. While it is not possible to directly quantify the 
number of augmented samples, a realistic estimate suggests that the 1388 training images 
effectively behave as tens of thousands of unique samples over multiple training epochs.

It is important to note that these sample numbers apply solely to training and validation. 
Once we are satisfied with the model's performance—measured through validation loss and 
accuracy—we retrain the models using the full training and validation sets, and 
finally, evaluate them on the testing set.

Since the goal is to develop models 
capable of generalizing well to unseen data for fire risk classification, it is 
crucial to prevent both overfitting and underfitting. To achieve this, 
we employ the early stopping technique, which monitors the validation loss and stops
training when no improvement is observed for a predefined number of epochs.
The predefined number of epochs for training ranges from 15 - 20. This range was
selected based on the epochs where the validation loss normally stopped decreasing.

Early stopping ensures that the model does not continue training unnecessarily when 
further improvement is unlikely. Specifically, after each epoch, the validation loss 
is compared with the lowest recorded loss up to that point. If the validation loss does 
not improve for five consecutive epochs (our patience parameter), training is 
stopped to prevent overfitting. In addition to saving the final model, we also save the 
model corresponding to the epoch with the best validation performance, ensuring that we 
retain the most effective version of the model.

Furthermore, accuracy and loss values for every epoch are recorded and stored in a JSON 
file for further analysis if required. After selecting the best-performing models based 
on validation loss, they are tested against the testing set to evaluate their 
ability to generalize to unseen data. The evaluation metrics used include 
the F1-score, accuracy, and the confusion matrix.

The trained models leverage transfer learning, where a pre-trained model serves 
as the base, and fine-tuning is applied to adapt it to the specific fire risk classification 
task. We initialize the models with default ImageNet weights and fine-tune them 
using our dataset.

The training is conducted using the AdamW optimizer with a custom learning rate 
scheduler in order to improve regularization. The learning rate is adjusted dynamically 
throughout the training process to improve convergence and stability.

During the first two epochs, a warm-up phase is applied, where the learning rate 
starts lower and gradually increases according to the following formula:

\[
\eta_t = \left(0.3 + 0.7 \times \frac{\text{epoch fraction}}{0.15} \right) \cdot \eta_0
\]

where \(\eta_t\) is the learning rate at epoch \(t\), and \(\eta_0\) is the initial learning rate.

After the warm-up phase, the learning rate is set to its initial value and remains constant 
during the main training phase until reaching the midpoint of the total training epochs. At 
this point, a cosine annealing decay is applied to gradually reduce the learning rate, ensuring 
stable convergence. The decay follows the equation:

\[
\eta_t = 0.5 \times \left(1 + \cos \left(\pi \times \frac{\text{epoch fraction} - 0.5}{0.5} \right) \right) \cdot \eta_0
\]


This scheduling strategy allows for an initial exploration phase, a stable learning 
period, and a smooth decay, ultimately leading to better generalization. 

Prior to adopting this adaptive learning rate strategy, static learning rates were 
tested. However, they led to overfitting, as the models quickly adapted to the training 
data but failed to generalize to unseen samples. The impact of this overfitting will be 
further analyzed in the Results and Discussion section. In addition, a gradual 
unfreezing strategy is applied to the model's layers during training to further 
reduce overfitting.

The loss function used depends on the number of classes. If there are two classes, 
BCEWithLogitsLoss is applied. Otherwise, CrossEntropyLoss is used for multi-class 
classification.

The models can also be trained with the NDVI index as an additional input channel, 
calculated from the IRG images, if the corresponding flag is enabled. This feature 
allows the model to leverage additional information about vegetation health and density.

To ensure robustness and a more realistic evaluation, the training process is repeated 
multiple times with different training and testing splits. The models are trained 
using a batch size of 16.


\subsection{Testing}

The evaluation of the models is carried out using different metrics, namely the confusion 
matrix, f1-score, and accuracy, measured on the test set. The confusion matrix provides 
insight into the performance of the model in distinguishing between different fire risk 
levels. By presenting classification results in a tabular format, it allows for a detailed 
analysis of which risk categories the model predicts more accurately and where misclassifications occur.

Once the models have been trained and validated, they are evaluated on a separate dataset, 
the test set, which serves as an independent benchmark for assessing generalization performance. 
This set ensures that the model is tested on unseen data, simulating real-world conditions 
where the classification of fire risk for isolated houses must be accurate and reliable.

In addition to evaluating models individually, a final classification is obtained through 
a \textit{majority voting} approach. Each model contributes an independent prediction for 
a given sample, and the final class is determined by selecting the most frequently predicted 
category among them. This ensemble decision-making process enhances robustness and mitigates 
individual model biases, leading to a more stable and reliable fire risk classification.

By using these evaluation methods, we can determine whether the trained models effectively 
extract meaningful information from aerial imagery and whether they are capable of correctly 
classifying fire risk levels in previously unseen data. This final assessment ensures that 
the proposed approach is suitable for real-world applications in fire risk prediction for isolated 
houses.
