\section{Motivation}
In recent years, the impact of climate change has become increasingly visible in the
form of more frequent and severe weather events, including wildfires \cite{unep2022wildfires}.
These fires pose a significant threat to ecosystems, properties, and human lives, particularly
when they occur at the wildland-urban interface.

In Portugal, the management of fuels around buildings, initially regulated by Decree-Law
124/2006 of June 28th \cite{portugal2006}, has been continually updated over the years with the most recent
update made by Decree-Law 82/2021 of October 13th \cite{portugal2021}\cite{houserefuge2021}\cite{houserefuge2024}. This law mandates
fuel management within a minimum radius of 50m around buildings. Fuel management involves
strategic measures such as conversion, reduction, and elimination of vegetation to decrease
fire intensity and spread rate. Some of the specific requirements include the buildings to
maintain a minimum distance of 5m between trees/shrubs and themselves and ensuring that
tree canopies are at least 4m apart from each other. Which is summarized in Figure \ref{fig:veg-man}.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{../Figures/Veg-management.png}
    \caption{Summary of main legal specifications relative to management of fuels in the surroundings of a house \cite{icnf1}\cite{icnf2}\cite{icnf3}.}
    \label{fig:veg-man}
\end{figure}

Reducing wildfire risks in residential areas heavily relies on effective vegetation management
in their vicinity. Traditional risk assessment methods are labor-intensive and prone
to human error, involving extensive fieldwork and manual data analysis. In contrast, machine
learning, particularly deep learning, presents a transformative approach to this challenge.
By automating the analysis of satellite/aerial imagery to classify vegetation risk, machine
learning offers faster, more accurate assessments at a larger scale. This capability is particularly
valuable for continuously updating our understanding of risk as landscapes change over time due
to both human activities and natural processes. Moreover, authorities can rapidly identify areas
where vegetation management requirements may not be met. This capability not only aids
in enforcing compliance with fire safety regulations but also improves proactive measures
to mitigate wildfire risks in order to protect lives and properties.

\section{Background}

The \gls{adai} conducted a comprehensive
national survey in 2020, covering over 2,000 isolated house locations to identify various risk
classes associated with the surrounding vegetation \cite{almeida2020analise}. The
distribution of the selection was based on the area burned in each district between 2016 and 2020, according to data
from the ICNF (Instituto de Conservação da Natureza e das Florestas). Inside each district, the
houses selection was random, while guaranteeing its distribution over the entire district area in order
to ensure a representative sample.

The study comprised of characterizing vegetation management within the immediate surroundings
(<50m) of the isolated houses. The evaluation was based on satellite images from 2018 to 2020 \cite{almeida2020analise}.
This study focuses primarily on the surroundings as a means of fire propagation, considering
only the structure of the existing vegetation, and excluding other factors such as orography.
The natural fuels can be classified as spontaneous or non-spontaneous. Spontaneous fuels are
those found in wildlands, while non-spontaneous fuels originate from human activities, such as
lawns, gardens, or vegetable plots. The natural fuels included for the fire risk
classification are categorized into three groups: arboreal, shrub, and herbaceous.

\begin{figure}[ht]

    \caption{Description of the characteristics of the immediate surroundings of the house and their relationship with the risk categories adapted from \cite{almeida2020analise}.}

There are five categories of fire risk defined: low, moderate, high, very high, and extreme. Given
the heterogeneity of vegetation around the houses, the determination of distance involved several
categories: trees, bushes, herbaceous plants, and continuous forest. The distance between
the main building and vegetation was measured for each of these categories, always considering
the nearest distance for each.

The Figure \ref{fig:fire-risk-table} shows the classification of fire risk based
on the distance between the vegetation and the main building.

It is important to notice that only vegetation that poses an effective fire risk was considered.
For instance, well-maintained and watered lawns were excluded from this analysis as they do not
have the capacity to propagate fire.

\section{Objectives}
The primary goal of this dissertation is to develop a robust machine learning classifier
that accurately identifies vegetation risk classes near isolated residences based on satellite
imagery and aerial photographs, adhering to \gls{adai}'s risk classification standards. To
achieve this, we will start by gathering and preprocessing relevant data, which includes
high-resolution aerial/satellite photographs from sources such as the \gls{dgt}, open API satellites, and the house labeling data from \gls{adai}.

Next, we will explore popular machine learning models and architectures for our problem at hand.
We will fine-tune these models to achieve the best possible performance, considering
the trade-offs between accuracy and computational complexity.

To sum up, this research aims to provide a scalable and efficient solution for assessing fire risk
around isolated houses, which can be used to inform strategic planning and community
safety initiatives. By automating the process of identifying vegetation risk classes, we
can help reduce the time and effort required to assess these fire risks, enabling more timely
and effective risk mitigation strategies. For example, authorities can use this tool in
conjunction with updated satellite imagery to identify, as fast as possible, areas where vegetation management
requirements are not met, and take action to enforce compliance with fire safety regulations, to
mitigate wildfires. Not only is this faster and possibly more accurate than traditional methods,
it also allows for a more proactive approach to the fuels management.
